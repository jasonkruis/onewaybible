You&rsquo;ve read the passage. You understand, more or less, what it meant back then &mdash; what Paul was telling the Corinthians, what Moses was telling Israel. And now comes the hard part, the part that makes people stare at a blank page on Saturday night: *what does this have to do with us?*

Most of us were taught to answer that question by **building a bridge**. The text sits on one side of a valley, in the ancient world. You sit on the other side, in the present. So you find the timeless principle underneath the passage &mdash; strip away the sandals and the Roman roads and the temple sacrifices until you&rsquo;re left with something general enough to cross over &mdash; and then you carry that principle across the bridge and apply it on your side.

It feels responsible. It feels careful. And it quietly ruins the way you read the Bible.

## Why the bridge goes wrong

Here&rsquo;s the problem with the bridge. To build it, *you* have to be the one who decides what the timeless principle is. You strip the passage down until it&rsquo;s abstract enough to travel &mdash; and in doing that, you leave behind almost everything the text actually said. &ldquo;Gouge out your eye if it causes you to sin&rdquo; becomes &ldquo;deal radically with temptation.&rdquo; That sounds like progress. But notice what happened: the vivid, specific, uncomfortable thing Jesus *said* got traded for a tame generality *you* constructed. And once you&rsquo;re the one building the bridge, you can build it anywhere you like. The text stops steering. You start steering, and the text just comes along for the ride.

That&rsquo;s why bridge-built sermons so often end up saying whatever the preacher already wanted to say. It&rsquo;s not because preachers are dishonest. It&rsquo;s because the method hands them the wheel.

## A different picture: the overlap

So try a different picture. Not a bridge between two separate places, but an **overlap** &mdash; two circles that share a great deal of ground.

Here is the key idea, and everything hangs on it: **the text was already reaching for you.** When God gave those words, He was not speaking only to the people in front of Him. He was speaking to a human heart &mdash; and your heart is a human heart. The sin the passage confronts is the sin you carry. The God it reveals is the God you answer to. The salvation it holds out is the salvation you need. The original hearers and you are not on opposite sides of a valley. You are standing in the *same field* &mdash; the field of being a sinner before a holy God who speaks &mdash; and the text&rsquo;s aim runs straight through that shared field and lands on both of you at once.

You don&rsquo;t build a bridge to the text. You **recognize where its aim already lands.**

That changes the whole task. You&rsquo;re no longer manufacturing relevance by abstraction. You&rsquo;re receiving a word that was pointed at you the whole time. The application isn&rsquo;t something you *add* to the text. It&rsquo;s something you *see* in it.

## &ldquo;Your phone is your right eye&rdquo;

Take the hard saying again. *If your right eye causes you to sin, tear it out and throw it away* <span class="ref">(Matthew 5:29)</span>.

The bridge method abstracts it: *deal seriously with sin.* Fine, true, and forgettable.

The overlap method does something else. It asks: what is the text actually reaching for? Jesus takes the most valuable, useful, *innocent-in-itself* thing &mdash; your own eye &mdash; and says if even *that* is the occasion of your sin, it must go. The scandal of the saying is precisely that the eye is not evil. It&rsquo;s good. And it still has to go, if it&rsquo;s the thing dragging you down.

Now stand in the shared field and ask what, in your life, occupies that exact place &mdash; valuable, useful, innocent in itself, and yet the occasion of your sin. For a great many people today, the honest answer is the phone in their pocket. So the application is not the pale &ldquo;deal seriously with sin.&rdquo; It is the text&rsquo;s own aim, landing: *your phone is your right eye.* If it is the thing pulling you into sin, it has to go &mdash; and the discomfort you feel at that sentence is the same discomfort Jesus&rsquo; first hearers felt, because it&rsquo;s the same word doing the same work.

Notice: nobody built a bridge. Nobody swapped the vivid thing for a tame one. The specific reach of the text simply arrived at a specific target in your world. That&rsquo;s the overlap.

## How to actually do it &mdash; four honest questions

This is not a license to make the text say anything. The whole point is that the text steers, not you. So before you land an application, run it through four questions. They don&rsquo;t *create* the connection &mdash; they *check* one you think you&rsquo;ve found, to make sure it&rsquo;s really the text&rsquo;s aim and not your own idea in the text&rsquo;s clothing.

**1. Does the connection come from the text&rsquo;s own point?**
Is the thing you&rsquo;re landing on genuinely what the passage was pressing &mdash; something in its actual words and argument &mdash; or something you brought and pinned on? The phone-as-eye passes because Jesus&rsquo; own point is *the valuable-but-ruinous thing must go*; the phone fits that shape exactly. If you have to reshape the passage to make your point fit, stop &mdash; that&rsquo;s a bridge, not an overlap.

**2. Does anything about the situation rule it out?**
Even a real connection can be misapplied. Does the specific case in front of you actually match, or is there a difference the text itself would flag? (An eye you can&rsquo;t remove isn&rsquo;t the same as a habit you can.) Check that the shoe truly fits before you make someone wear it.

**3. Does the action really follow &mdash; or are you overreaching?**
The text warrants *what it warrants*, and not one inch more. &ldquo;Get rid of the phone that&rsquo;s dragging you into sin&rdquo; follows. &ldquo;Therefore all phones are sinful&rdquo; does not &mdash; that&rsquo;s you riding the text past its own stopping point. Land exactly where the text lands; don&rsquo;t use its momentum to carry your hobby-horse.

**4. Are you ignoring the Bible&rsquo;s other words on this?**
One true command, wielded while you suppress every balancing word, becomes a lie. Don&rsquo;t press this text so hard that you drown out what Scripture says elsewhere about the same situation. Let all the relevant words have their say.

If an application clears all four, you can say it with confidence &mdash; not because you&rsquo;re clever, but because you&rsquo;re not the source of it. You found it; you didn&rsquo;t build it.

## Say it like you mean it &mdash; and hold it with humility

Here is the strange freedom in all this. When you preach or counsel this way, your authority actually *goes up* and your bossiness *goes down* at the same time.

Up, because you&rsquo;re not offering your opinion. You&rsquo;re handing someone a word God aimed at them. You can say it plainly.

Down, because the specific target &mdash; *this* phone, *this* habit, *this* relationship &mdash; involves your fallible read of their situation, and you know it. So the honest way to land it isn&rsquo;t &ldquo;God says get rid of your phone, full stop, because I said so.&rdquo; It&rsquo;s closer to: **&ldquo;I think you&rsquo;ll find that Jesus is telling you to get rid of your phone.&rdquo;** That&rsquo;s not weakness or hedging. The text&rsquo;s aim is certain; *your* aim of it at their particular life is where humility belongs. You hand the verdict back to them and to the Spirit, who can confirm in their own heart what you could only point at.

That is application on the Bible&rsquo;s terms. You stop building bridges out of your own material. You stand in the same field the first hearers stood in, you find where the text&rsquo;s aim already lands, you check it honestly, and then you say it &mdash; plainly, because it&rsquo;s God&rsquo;s word, and humbly, because you&rsquo;re only the one pointing.

The text was always reaching for you. The work was never to build a way across. It was to stop building, look up, and see where it was pointing all along.
