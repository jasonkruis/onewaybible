There&rsquo;s a sentence I keep coming back to, and it sounds strange the first time you hear it: *the only time I&rsquo;m ever right is when I say I&rsquo;m wrong and God is right.*

Read quickly, it sounds like false modesty &mdash; the kind of thing you say to seem humble. But it isn&rsquo;t about seeming anything. It&rsquo;s about the deepest posture of the human heart, and whether that posture is bent toward God or away from him. And the book of the Bible that teaches it most clearly is probably the oldest one of all: Job.

## A good man with one problem

We tend to remember Job as the book about suffering, and it is. But watch what actually happens to Job&rsquo;s *heart* over the course of it, because that&rsquo;s the real story.

Job starts out genuinely good. God says so himself &mdash; blameless, upright, a man who fears God and turns from evil <span class="ref">(Job 1:1)</span>. This is important: Job is not a secret sinner getting what he deserves. He&rsquo;s a truly righteous man. So whatever goes wrong in him over the next forty chapters, it isn&rsquo;t the ordinary wickedness his friends keep accusing him of.

Then the suffering comes, wave after wave, and Job&rsquo;s friends show up with their explanations. And slowly, under the pressure of pain he can&rsquo;t make sense of, something shifts in Job. He starts to reason like this: *I know I&rsquo;m righteous. And look what&rsquo;s happening to me. So either I&rsquo;m wrong about myself&hellip; or God is wrong in how he&rsquo;s treating me.* And Job, cornered, begins to choose the second. He says God has wronged him <span class="ref">(Job 19:6)</span>. He says God has taken away his right <span class="ref">(Job 27:2)</span>. He wants his day in court, certain that if he could just make his case, he&rsquo;d be vindicated and God would be shown to be in the wrong.

Do you see what&rsquo;s happened? The good man has quietly seated himself as the judge of God. He has made his own certainty about himself the measuring stick, and he&rsquo;s prepared to find God guilty by it. *I am right, and God is wrong.*

## The oldest sin, in a new setting

If that move sounds familiar, it should. It&rsquo;s the very first thing that ever went wrong.

In the garden, the serpent&rsquo;s offer was not mainly about a piece of fruit. It was an offer to become the one who decides &mdash; to see for yourself, judge for yourself, declare good and evil for yourself, instead of receiving all of that from God. And the fatal moment came when Eve *looked* at the tree and *declared it good* <span class="ref">(Genesis 3:6)</span> &mdash; taking to herself a verdict that belonged to God alone. The creature set itself up as the independent knower, the final judge.

That&rsquo;s the same move Job is making, just in the register of suffering instead of fruit. He&rsquo;s trusting his own sight, his own certainty, his own verdict &mdash; over God&rsquo;s. And notice: it&rsquo;s not that Job&rsquo;s facts are all wrong. He really is righteous; his friends really are talking nonsense half the time. The problem isn&rsquo;t that Job has bad information. The problem is *the posture* &mdash; the settled confidence that his own read of things is the standard by which even God can be judged. That&rsquo;s the broken heart underneath. Not ignorance. Not even, at first, obvious rebellion. Just the quiet, deep certainty that *I* am the one who gets to say what&rsquo;s right.

And here is the sobering thing: that posture can live perfectly well inside a genuinely good person. Job proves it. You can fear God, turn from evil, be blameless among your neighbors &mdash; and still carry, down at the root, the Edenic assumption that when push comes to shove, your own judgment is the final court. Most of us don&rsquo;t find out it&rsquo;s there until something breaks.

## What actually heals it

So how does God fix this in Job? Not the way we&rsquo;d expect.

God never explains himself. When he finally speaks, out of the whirlwind, he doesn&rsquo;t give Job the answer to his suffering. He doesn&rsquo;t lay out the scene in heaven, or the reasons, or the plan. He asks Job questions &mdash; where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? &mdash; question after question that Job cannot answer, until it becomes overwhelmingly clear that Job is a creature standing before a Creator whose wisdom and rule are simply beyond him.

And that&rsquo;s what does it. Not information &mdash; Job gets none. What changes Job is being brought back to his place: creature before Creator, dependent knower before the only independent One. And out of that, Job says the thing the whole book was driving toward: *I have heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you; therefore I despise myself, and repent in dust and ashes* <span class="ref">(Job 42:5&ndash;6)</span>.

That&rsquo;s the reversal. That&rsquo;s the broken heart mended. Job stops being the judge. He gives the verdict back to God. He says, in effect, *I was wrong, and you are right* &mdash; not because his questions got answered, but because he stopped demanding to be the one who answers them. The healing wasn&rsquo;t an explanation. It was a return to dependence.

## Why this is the deepest thing about you

Here is why all of this matters, and why it isn&rsquo;t just an interesting reading of an old book.

The posture Job had to unlearn is the posture underneath *everything* in the Christian life. It&rsquo;s underneath how you read the Bible &mdash; do you receive what it says, or sit in judgment over it and keep the parts you like? It&rsquo;s underneath how you handle being corrected &mdash; do you soften and turn, or defend the courtroom in which you are always the judge? It&rsquo;s underneath repentance itself, every single time: to repent is simply to say, again, *I was wrong and God is right*, and to mean it with your whole self. Every act of real obedience is a small version of Job&rsquo;s ending &mdash; the creature laying down its claim to be the final court, and depending.

Which is why the strange sentence turns out to be exactly right. *The only time I&rsquo;m ever right is when I say I&rsquo;m wrong and God is right.* Not because I&rsquo;m always mistaken about the facts. But because the one posture that is ever truly *right* &mdash; the one that isn&rsquo;t the old Edenic lie wearing a respectable face &mdash; is the posture of the dependent creature who has stopped judging God and started trusting him. Everything good grows from that root. Job found it at the bottom of his suffering. Most of us find it the same way.

And when you do &mdash; when the certainty finally breaks and you say it and mean it &mdash; you&rsquo;ll find it isn&rsquo;t the crushing thing you feared. It&rsquo;s the first free breath. The judge&rsquo;s bench was never a comfortable place to sit. It was only ever a lonely one. Coming down from it, back to being a creature who can simply trust his Maker, is the beginning of rest.
